** /The Odyssey/, Christopher Nolan, 2026

The most interesting part of the film for me was the closing shot: the ship sailing off into the setting sun. Nolan uses it to gesture at the end of civilization, and on the surface it reads as carrying a small, cyclical optimism — civilizations rise, fall, and presumably rise again.

That reading is a cope, though. The cyclical framing is actually deeply pessimistic, not optimistic: it says a civilization can never progress past some ceiling before collapsing again, forever. That is /historicism/ in Popper's sense — a deterministic prediction about the necessary course of history — and history isn't determined like that. It could stay static, go in cycles, or progress without any upper bound. Having science at all is precisely the reason to expect the latter; without it there would be no such reason.

/Xenia/ (guest-right/hospitality) functions in the film as something close to a Kantian universal law, and the plot turns on its violation. Odysseus wins the war with the Trojan Horse — an intellectual trick that outsmarts Athena, the goddess of wisdom herself, whom he betrays in the act — but the trick also destroys hospitality and trust between people. The concept of the Trojan Horse is itself a trojan horse: it looks like clever short-term strategy, while undermining the much larger, slower strategy — civilizational ethics — that everything else actually depends on.

The film never states it, but the implied consequence is that the single most relevant political goal has to be something like a lasting world peace: preventing civilizational breakdown. That's the unstated solution to the problem the film poses — which is also why the film reads as pessimistic to me: it treats the breakdown as near-inevitable instead of naming the actual, available alternative.

** /Suicidal Empathy: Dying to Be Kind/, Gad Saad, 2025

Must-read if you're interested in this phenomenon.

Saad's framing: maladaptively irrational altruism as a parasitic, essentially anti-adaptive meme that hijacks / mis-adapts the empathy submodule of the psyche — leading to a catastrophic miscalibration of moral priorities (coddling violent criminals, protecting rapists, branding self-defense as toxic).

Overlaps strongly with [[https://www.danisulikowski.com/][Dani Sulikowski]]'s work — I think they're looking at the same phenomenon, but she has the more differentiated, more powerful account. Saad's is basically right but underspecified: it names the mechanism (parasitic meme hijacks empathy module) without explaining why civilizations commit this kind of self-sabotage in the first place. Sulikowski's explanation is embedded further in evolutionary theory and addresses that "why" directly, which makes it the stronger of the two.

A strange mistake in the field of philosophy is how ideas are attached to the thinker; There is a misconception there.

In science, it is obvious that a theory is not "best understood" by it's discoverer.
Darwin and Watson are not an authority on how evolution works, in fact they were plagued by misconceptions and missing knowledge.
They didn't know about genetics, Watson was mistaken about sexual selection, etc.

Reading Darwin is interesting for a historian of science, only secondary for the evolutionary theorist.
The same is true for Popperian epistomelogy. So far the best intro I know to it are David Deutsch's books.

Saying "You should understand Darwin" as synomym to "understand evolution by natural selection" would be a kind of pessimism.

It would be equivalent to stating "The theory is a marshmallow, it's structure is arbitrary and the only true instance of 'understanding' it
was in Darwin's mind when he wrote the book".

It would say

1. Reality is not out there for us to understand.
2. Ergo, no error correction towards understanding reality is available.
3. The structure of ideas has nothing to do with the structure of reality, you must understand ideas in their own right.
4. The thinker of an idea cannot be wrong by any criterion.

This is such a strange set of statements.

** ⛏ /Compositional Evolution/ Richard A. Watson, 2006

(currently reading).

I thought it is a genetics algorithm book, then I started reading.

What is being proposed is profound.

That the algorithmic structure of natural selection is not /hill climbing/ but /devide and conquer/.

The whole project of analyzing natural selection in terms of algorithmic principles already is a beatiful
well chosen layer between computation and biology.

Watson reviews the genetic algorithm (GA) literature with profound density.

Then develops a simple algorithm based on symbiotic encapsulation that does well
in problems with /modular structure/.

----------------------

This book changed my mind about *the* core apect of my world view, the Dennett + Dawkins Darwinian view of life and knowledge - remarkable!

The theory of evolution is the most profound explanation within biology.

In Dennett's view, 'Darwinism' is generalized to the notion that everthing complicated must be made from simpler stuff.
For Deutsch, /abstract replicator theory/ is one of the 4 strands of reality, and says how biology is related to epistemology - knowledge creation.

Richard Watson updates not the notion of 'Darwinism', but changes our understanding of the /algorithmic structure of natural selection/.


*gradualism*: The position that variation in natural selection must be 'incremental and small'. That is small mutations in the genetic material.

*updated view*: Compositional (Sex, symbiotic encapsulation, ...) effects are not 'gradual', but relevant for /what kind of systems are evolvable/.

In defence of the essential view of /gradualism/, I would say a /generalized gradualism/ is a kind of 'causal contingency principle';
You /can/ make a leap in genotype space via symbiosis/modularity (new). But you cannot make a leap in the causal web of physics (old).

I'm certain Darwin, Dennett and the living Dawkins would agree and find joy in this view!

No surprise that they treat their chidren as full persons (like [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taking_Children_Seriously][TCS]]).

You either solve your problem or die, very Darwinian.
They believe in spirits but those are the animals and so forth they encounter or something.
Either way, just read the book.

For Everett, the ability to tell a story is is central, not grammer. Generative (Chomskian) linguistics elevated the sentence to
the unit of language, but in reality it is the document / story.

Everett points out that Pirahã stories feature recursion.
This suggests that people have complex cognitive representations, without the need for the language constructs to reflect this.

Another simple observation is that in everyday language, we don't rely on infinite recursive structure to say the things we talk about.

The weaker version is combinatorical explosion. Wilhelm v. Humboldt "The infite use of finite means".
It's fine if the number of allowed meanings is practically infinite coming from an exponential explotion.

Language doesn't need an infinity of meaning to work - just a practical infinity.

This is a departure from the pure math world down into a kind of embodied domain.

The same might be neccessary in software design and biological computing too.
To move forward from prestine absolute truths, and into a realm of ad-hoc guesses, best efforts, unreliable substrates etc.
We will be giving up some tools but the promise is that we will have a theory of creativity in hand afterwards.

** Sara Imari Walker /Life As No One Knows It: The Physics of Life's Emergence/, 2024

It seems to be there are some new kinds of people.
That want to make a science and physics that takes higher level concepts seriously.

/Emergence/ not as a conversation stopper;
But a science were emergent properties are as fundamental as particles or something in current physics.

'Fundamental' is a property of your explanation, not a property of the size of the thing you talk about.


=Assembly= is a meassure of 'depth in causality' of a physical object.

Measuring assembly is looking at how many building blocks you recursively create from the primitive building blocks.
Once a block is created, it can be reused.

This is algorithmically similar to compression (and it is a scientific dispute whether it provides additional insight, see wikipedia).

It is a sympathetic aspect of assembly theory that this can be meassured with the humble mass spectroscopy;
Shooting a molecule into smaller molecules, then arranging them in a path and counting the steps.

I feel like these building blocks are a /chemistry/ idea to have.

Trying to make software /from/ that is challanging, because one of Saras main points is that real /chemistry/ needs to do the search process.

The closest software paradigm that comes to mind to me is /relational programming/, and I think the
the most relevant concept of assembly theory should be the *constraint*.

Because all possible combinations of building blocks, called /assembly universe/, is exponentially large.

And modeling the /assembly universe/ is algorithmically equivalent to /exhaustive search/.
The challange is to describe *constraints*, that make /assembly posssible/ and /assembly contingent/ out of the universal space.

A Hebbian Cell Assembly (Hebb 1949), is an auto associative datastructure,

*auto associative*: A memory regime where a value can be looked up with a version of itself,
implementing a /pattern complete/.

It can be implemented in a spiking, recurrent neuronal net with global inhibition
where subnetworks of neurons have more connectivity to itself, thereby producing 'hubs' of activity that stabilize itself (they form attractors in a dynamical system view).

"Igniting" part of the assembly retrieves the rest.

*plasticity*: A mechanim that modifies the excitability properties of neurons in a neuronal net.
This could be done by changing the excitability of neurons (non-Hebbian), changing weights or counts of synapses.

*plasticity rule*: An algorithmic rule, usually part of the /update rule/ for a neuronal net that specifies a plasticity.
The best known plasticity rule is called /Hebbian learning/, that reads 'for all synapses where the source neuron was active in the last step and the target neuron is active in the current step, update the synaptic weight by a plasticity factor β'.
It is summarized as 'neurons that fire together wire together'.

Given some form of plasticity that makes sure that 'what is active together associates', we can arrange 3 neuronal areas such that there are some random connections
between all of them.

An ensemble spanning the 3 areas forms and via inputs in sensor and motor area, it is related to the world.
(How and why can be said with math. Here is an approachable model and great talk: [[https://youtu.be/mSX9CCKdBDA?si=CEzGSSdIVzaZP6qX][The Assembly Hypothesis:Emergent Computation and Learning in a rigorous model of the Brain]]).

In the case of speech, a child might acquire an ensemble spanning association and sensor area, after hearing a phoneme.
(Note that which neurons contribute is completely random, it just happens to be a subnetwork that was randomly associated the most with the input data).

The child could then via some sort of learning behaviour, try to make random motor outputs (babble), until an ensemble is found that feeds back from both it's connectivity
and via sensor input.
I.e. a motor ensemble that is stabilized in turn by the sound-data coming into the sensory area.

Once such an arrangement is grown, the system could use the association area to bring back motor and sensor data associated with the phoneme.
And the complete ensemble is allowed to /stand for/ the phoneme.

It sort of looks as if the association area in this arrangement is representing a 'concept', into which other areas could hook into.
([[https://youtu.be/X9roMB8nCbo?si=4lq3DeUAqk8fixvd][Pulvermueller: Semantic grounding of concepts and meaning in brain-constrained neural networks]]).


Given this implementation,

1. Lip reading is using the motor inputs (incidentally, 'mirror neurons') to retrieve at least the =A= - part of phonemes,
   since the =A= part of phonemes already is the 'concept' of that phoneme, the rest of the brain can continue with information processing
   as if we were listen to speech.
   - this model explains why lip reading is not so much a special skill, but a tuning in to the motor aspect of how we percieve speech in the first place.
     No further reconstruction needs be done, it should be a kind of silent perception of speech.

2. McGurk effect would be explainend by the sensory area ensemble =S= being ambigous enough so that actually the motor area part =M= has the greater influence on =A=.
   "Flipping" the attractor states of the ensemble in =A=, igniting the =A= part associated with either 'fa' or 'ba'.
   - once the ball rolls into the valey of either 'fa' or 'ba', it stabilizes itself at =S=, too. I say the ensemble 'spans' the areas.
     Presumably, the activation at =S= is involved in the perception of 'hearing' the phoneme.

3. Looking at a persons mouth while they speak helps /hearing/, because the signal from both =M= and =S= ignites the correct =A= reliably.

** /Lights On/, Annaka Harris, 2025

This audio production has it's interesting parts, but fails to mine anything new.

*conscious experience*: The property of 'it feels like to be' (T. Nagel) a system.
Consciousness is sometimes used as synomym for 'cognition' or 'mentality'; Which can be analysed in terms of software modules. I.e. the
mind as the software running on the brain. Analyzing its function is Chalmers 'easy problem', opposed to the 'hard problem', which I call '(phenomenological) consciousness'.

*qualia*: Hypothetical elements out of which conscious experience is made out of.
E.g. the 'sensation of blue'.

There is no explanatory theory connecting (biological) information processing, physics, behavior science / neuroscience and the problem of consciousness.

*emergentism*: The idea that consciousness is a property of the interaction of physical submodules / elements.
Daniel Dennett doesn't use the word. He calls the idea of complex things being made of simpler things /gradualism/ or /Darwinism/ (Dennett 1995).

*emergence*: The property of a /complex system/ that allows it to be /explained/ in terms of a /simpler/ set of explanations.

David Deutsch's example is explaining the copper atom of the nose of a statue of a president in honor of winning some war.
The /sub-emergent/ explanation in terms of atoms is virtually useless. The emergent explanation in terms of social structures, wars,
status, the concept of a leader, honor etc. Is more useful.

Biology, psychology, cybernetics / software engineering are the canonical disciplines dealing with emergent properties of systems;
Common concetps are information, knowledge, replicators (memes/genes), adaptation, error correction, communication / signaling, top down causation, self-organisation.

*illusionism*: The idea that consciousness is created by a 'pretend' / 'what if' process. A better name would be 'virtualism'.
A common misconception is that illusionism says 'consciousness does not exist'. This is not correct. Illusionism holds that consciousness exists,
but that it is not what it appears to be.

Story:

Physist A: Why did people believe the world was flat?

Physist B: Because it looked like it was flat.

Physist A: How would it have looked if it was a globe?

The answer is, exactly like 'this'. In Illusionism, we say that consciousness looks like /xyz/, exactly how it would look like if it was a virtual software system.

*virtual*: Of an information processing system: The property of being 'pretend' or 'as if'. We say of a virtual system that it exists 'on top of' an /abstraction barrier/.

*(vertical) abstraction barrier*: In Software engineering, expressing a /higher level/ sub module in terms of abstractions - A conventional language, also called 'short codes'.

A "layer of abstraction" is created by implementing an imaginary (virtual) machine in terms of a lower level language ('long codes').
The /implemenation/ must bottom out to hardware-level operations (circuits). Once this is in place, a system can be expressed /in terms of/ a higher level language, *as-if* the virtual machine (/the interpreter/) existed.

This is also called *stratified design*. The most central concept in software engineering is the *interpreter* (or /systems of expression/).

Daniel Dennetts favorite example is the Java Virtual Machine (JVM). The JVM is a virtual machine that can be implemented on different operating systems, bottoming out to
hardware instruction codes. Even though different OS'es have different instruction codes, on the level of the JVM, the same code runs on all of the computers for which
an implemenation of the JVM was programmed.

It is a bit like having a /lingua franca/, with a kind of /substance independence/. Only that the concept of /substance independence/ is scale-free.
In the case of the JVM, it is on the level of operating systems.

*abstraction*: Treating many things as if they were one thing. In the case of the JVM, treating many operating systems as if they were a single operating system,
namely the virtual OS of the JVM.

** ⛏ /The Science of Can And Can't/, Chiara Marletto, 2021

Wonderful! Now I have the fundamental notions of Constructor Theory in my mind. Woven together with some fabric
made out of the wonder of science and the world.

Constructor theory is a mode of explanation that talks about possible and impossible tasks.
Sounds innocent, but it's a whole philosophy that allows one to explain the world not only in what is /actual/,
but also what /could be/ - with /counterfactuals/.

This is very joyful and open-ended. For instance, information is about all the things that /could/ be said,
not about the stuff that /is/ said.

The universal constructor is a machine that can be programmed to perform any task, any physical transformation.
It divides the world into possible and impossible. Thereby, the repertoire of the universal constructor /is/ a
description of physical laws.

One might think, what about tasks you can't do because no material is at hand? Or some such intervention.
But then you realize that this is exactly what it means to be limited by physical laws.
For instance, you cannot go faster than light, you cannot create energy from anything, you cannot use the energy that went into heat and so forth.

This is equivalent to considering a different kind of /interpreter/, that can do not only make information processing tasks, but physical tasks, too.
Jon Von Neuman was on this quest to generalize the theory of computing in this way; Giving this project the name universal constructor.
Since computing devices bottom out at some physical reality, I think this roughly says that we can see reality as
an interpreter for a kind of constraint language of physicality.
Like 'if you put these particles next to each (syntax), then this and that will happen' and so forth (that is for physics to say).
The language/repertoire of the physical interpreter is equivalent to stating the laws of physics, and we arrived at the basic
the notion of constructor theory from a second angle.

So in some ways, constructor theory is a natural extension of the theory of computation and programming;
It is a great candidate for providing deeper theories of software, programming, and virtual simulated realities,
which I think are needed for theories of brain software, creativity, memetics and so forth.

My summary of the core of his [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention_schema_theory][thinking]] is such:

1. =Attention= is a thing.
   Arguably it is even sort of part of the most low-level substance of neuronal function. Consider how lateral inhibition is a basic attention mechanism.
2. High-level sensory /=schemas=/ are a thing.
   Consider how the map is not the territory and consider how the map is useful.
   Via programming consider abstractions - the usefulness of taking many things and saying they are one thing.
   The way it doesn't matter where the rocks lie on the territory - the interesting thing is the roads or such.
   From classical neuroscience, we have those body map schemas, our body sensors are abstracted away to a simple map.
   In other words, the mind is built out of maps - left, right and center.
   Interfaces, also called abstraction barriers in programming, hide the low-level, messy mechanisms of what is going on somewhere below, for instance in the engines of sensory physiology.
   So far I think Dennett agrees with you.
   Sub-resources, or sub-agents of the mind are interfacing via simplified information processing channels/walls/surfaces - call them maps or schemas because we called them like that in neuroscience.
   There are maps for vision, there are maps for pitch, there are maps for [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_perception][motion perception]].
   Maybe there are maps for the physical and the social and the belonging realm and maybe there are 10 realms and 1000 maps, who knows?
   This reasoning is where Dennett says "consciousness" is a /benign user illusion/. It is the highest level of interfaces with which the mind is interacting - with itself.
3. Humans have some high-level attention mechanisms, some resources that sort of guide /what the mind is doing/ from moment to moment.
   My mental model of this is like weather patterns if you zoom in. Saying /now we are paying attention to =x= /, /now we are imagining =y= / and such things.
4. The final twist is that we have /schema(s) for those attention mechanisms/. What he calls the Attention Schema (theory) AST.
   There is an abstract map, schema, interface, abstraction barrier, or user interface -
   With the target, the territory; the content of /attention/. Presumably sort of more or less global attention of what the mind is about from moment to moment.
   And then somehow the [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporoparietal_junction][TPJ]]  and STS are relevant for this to work - sort of fitting into the larger neuroscience explanatory picture of TPJ and [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemispatial_neglect][Hemispatial neglect]].
5. This user interface would feel completely insubstantial.
   It would be an abstraction /about/ something abstract.
   Consider the sensory body map, it does not feel like a body. It is a simplification. It is a *map*, not a territory of what a body is.
   But its target, its content is something physical - it is /about/ the body, which is physical.
   So even though body sensations are quite ephemeral we are not yet bothered too much with its insubstantial nature.
   But the attention schema has as its target not something physical in the world, but aspects of the /processing/ of the mind.
   What should the mind be about in the next moment? It has to do with the thoughts I had 10 seconds ago and the multilayered integration of information.
   It has to do with what my mind was about in the last couple of minutes, with my goals, my personality, my environment, and my sensory inputs.
   How does it feel to have a user interface to how the mind is spending its time from moment to moment?
   Graziano is arguing that this double abstraction, the fact that the attention schema is in turn /about/ contents of the mind,
   is why we feel our subjective experience is so ephemeral, or mystic in some or another way.

Some of the flavor of our subjective experience, some of the flow of our minds, is an inner representation of /attention/.
