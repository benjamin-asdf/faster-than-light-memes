A friend tells you about Michael Newton.
A hypnotherapist who, in /Journey of Souls/, regressed his clients past their birth,
past their previous death, into the place /in between/.[fn:newton]
They report a spirit world. Souls returning home, meeting their guides, their soul group,
standing before a council of elders, choosing the next body and the next life.[fn:newton-content]
Case after case, and by his account the reports are strikingly consistent.[fn:newton-consistent]

The usual way to deal with this is to weigh it.
What speaks for it? (Consistent reports, sincere people, a serious practitioner.)
What speaks against it? (A list just as long.)
And then you argue about Newton, his method, his clients, and nobody changes their mind.

I want to make a different move. I will not say a single word against Newton.

* What else would I have to believe?

Instead of /is this claim true?/, ask:

1. If I accept this /kind/ of evidence, what else /should/ I believe?
2. If somebody else comes with the same kind of evidence, what can /they/ claim?

This is Kant's move, transplanted.
Kant says: act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.[fn:kant]
You don't judge the single act, you judge the /rule/ behind it, as if everybody followed it.

So, the *maxim of evidence*:

#+begin_quote
Believe only according to that maxim of evidence which you could at the same time will to become a universal law of knowledge.
#+end_quote

The evidence here is not the claim. The evidence is:
/a vivid, meaningful, emotionally overwhelming experience, in a special state of mind (hypnosis, prayer, trance, meditation, a near-death experience, a drug),/
/whose content is then taken as a description of how reality is./

Let's universalize it. Here is who else gets to make claims, if that is a legitimate source of knowledge:

| Who                           | Experience                                | Therefore                                       |
|-------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------|
| a Christian                   | encounters Jesus in prayer                | Jesus is the risen son of God                   |
| a Muslim                      | feels the presence of Allah in salat      | there is no god but God, and Muhammad is His messenger |
| a Vaishnava devotee           | experiences Krishna in bhakti             | Krishna is the "Supreme Personality of Godhead" |
| an ancient Greek              | the oracle at Delphi speaks for Apollo    | Zeus and the Olympians rule the world           |
| a shaman on ayahuasca         | talks to the spirit of the plant          | plants have spirits that teach                  |
| someone on DMT                | meets "self-transforming machine elves"   | there is a hyperdimensional elf realm           |
| an abductee, under hypnosis   | recalls being examined on a ship          | aliens are visiting us                          |
| a Scientologist in auditing   | recalls lives on other planets            | we are thetans (and, at OT III, Xenu)           |
| a Buddhist meditator          | sees through the self                     | rebirth, but there is /no/ soul that wanders    |
| someone at 4am                | a glitch, a déjà vu, everything is too neat | we live in a simulation                       |
| Newton's client               | recalls the life between lives            | souls wander, through a spirit world            |

/The rows, checked:/[fn:table]

Every row has the same shape. Same kind of experience, same step from experience to ontology.
If the last row is legitimate, all of them are.

* They can't all be true

And now the trouble is obvious, just from making the list.

- One life, then judgement (mainstream Christianity, Islam) vs. many lives (reincarnation).[fn:one-life]
- A soul that travels (Newton) vs. no self at all that could travel (Buddhism).[fn:anatta]
- One god vs. many gods vs. no gods but elves vs. no gods but aliens.
- Jesus is the son of God (Christianity) vs. Jesus is a prophet and explicitly /not/ the son of God (Islam).[fn:islam-jesus]

The experiences do not merely differ in detail, they contradict each other.
But the /evidence/ is of the same quality in each case. The Christian's prayer is not less vivid than the hypnotized client's memory.
Nobody in this table is lying.

Evidence that supports X and not-X equally well supports neither.

So the conclusion is not /Newton is wrong/. The conclusion is:

#+begin_quote
If the Muslim says X, and the Christian says not-X, both on the basis of experiences of class Y,
then experiences of class Y are not a basis for knowledge.
#+end_quote

The experiences are real. They are real /experiences/. They are excellent data about minds,
about what brains do in trance, in prayer, under hypnosis, at the edge of death.
They are not data about the furniture of the universe.

* Popper: there are no authoritative sources

This is a special case of something Karl Popper said in /On the Sources of Knowledge and of Ignorance/:
the question /where does your knowledge come from?/ is the wrong question.
There are no ultimate, authoritative sources. Not the senses, not reason, not tradition. (And, I add: not the Bible, not the trance, not the expert.)[fn:popper]
Every source is welcome as a suggestion, and every source is open to criticism. Any of them can lead us into error.

Knowledge has no authoritative source. It is conjecture that has, so far, survived criticism.[fn:popper-conjecture]

Popper's replacement question is: /How can we hope to detect and eliminate error?/[fn:popper-question]
Applied to a claim: not /what is your source?/ but /how could we find out if you're wrong?/
For the table above, the honest answer is: we can't. Any outcome of any further session fits.

* Deutsch: hard to vary

David Deutsch gives the sharper tool (/The Beginning of Infinity/, chapter 1).[fn:deutsch]

In the usual retelling (Deutsch's too), the ancient Greeks explained winter with Persephone: Hades abducted her, she ate pomegranate seeds,
so she must spend part of each year in the underworld, and while she is gone her mother Demeter's grief leaves the earth barren and cold.[fn:persephone]

The problem isn't merely that the story is false. The problem is that it is /easy to vary/.[fn:easy-to-vary]
Replace Persephone with another goddess, the pomegranate with a fig, grief with anger - it explains winter just as well.
The details do no work. Nothing in the account is constrained by the thing it is supposed to explain.

Compare the axial tilt of the earth. Change any detail and the explanation breaks.
And it gets right what the myth gets wrong: when it is winter in Greece, it is summer in Australia.[fn:tilt]

Now apply that to the life between lives.

- Why do /souls/ wander? Why not /hearts/?
  The ancient Egyptians did put the self in the heart, and weighed it against a feather after death.[fn:heart]
  It would fit the hypnosis reports just as well.
- Why a council of elders? Why not a single judge, or 42 judges (also Egypt), or none?[fn:assessors]
- Why one soul per person? Why not seven, or a soul that splits?
- Why a spirit world in between? Why not straight into the next body? Why not angels of Christ who receive you? Why not Hermes leading you down to Charon's ferry?[fn:afterlife-guides]

Every detail could be swapped out, and the account would explain the reports equally well.
That is the tell. If an explanation can absorb any variant, it isn't constrained by reality,
it is constrained by what the person (and the person asking the questions) finds narratively plausible.
And that is exactly the variance we see across the table: each culture fills in the slots with its own furniture.

The variation /between/ the rows is the variation the explanation /permits/.

* How would it look if you were being tricked?

The rationalist says: when I see a mirage in the desert, it will look like an oasis.
That is what a mirage /is/. So "it looked exactly like an oasis" is no evidence for an oasis.

So ask: /how would it look to somebody who is being tricked?/
Exactly like this. Vivid, real, meaningful, more real than real.
If the experience would look the same whether the god exists or not, the experience can't tell you which.
When I experience a god, the best explanation of my experience is psychology, not the existence of a god.

Think of a magic trick. To the one being tricked it looks like magic. That is the whole point of a good trick.
"I saw it with my own eyes" is not an objection to the trick, it is what the magician designed.

And here is the part I like most: the explanation of the trick is /more/ interesting than magic.
"Magic" explains nothing; it is the easiest-to-vary explanation there is.
The real explanation needs misdirection, attention, timing, years of training,
and a deep, practical knowledge of how perception works and how it fails.
That is where the depth is.

The same goes for the god in the prayer, the soul in the trance, the elf on DMT.
The explanation lives in psychology and neuroscience: how brains build experience out of expectation,
how attention, suggestion, ritual and practice shape what we feel.
(Tanya Luhrmann, in /When God Talks Back/, describes how evangelical Christians practise prayer as a skill until God feels real.)[fn:luhrmann]

Saying "it's psychology" is not a dismissal. It is the harder, deeper, more interesting explanation.
"A god did it" is the boring one.

* But the reports are so consistent!

This is the strongest-sounding argument for any claim of this kind, so let's apply the same move to it.
If consistency of reports were good evidence, what else would it prove?

Look at the table again. Every row is consistent.
Christians consistently meet Jesus, not Krishna. Vaishnavas consistently meet Krishna, not Jesus.
DMT users report strikingly similar entities. Abductees report strikingly similar ships and examinations.[fn:table]
Studies of deathbed visions in the US and in India (Osis and Haraldsson, /At the Hour of Death/) found the same basic pattern in both,
"someone comes to fetch you", but /who/ comes follows the culture:
Americans mostly saw deceased relatives, Indians more often religious figures, like the messengers of Yama.[fn:osis]

If consistency were the test, every row would pass, including the rows that contradict each other.
The consistency is /within/ a tradition, and the differences are /between/ traditions.

So ask again: how would it look if the experience were shaped by what the experiencer brings into it? Exactly like this.
Anything that produces experiences of this kind has plenty of sources of consistency that need no other world:

- A shared method: the same ritual, the same practice, the same questions. Questions carry their framework in with them.
- Shared expectations: whoever seeks out the prayer meeting, the ceremony or the session has usually heard what it is supposed to look like.
- Suggestible states: trance, hypnosis, ecstasy. Hypnosis makes people recall more, and more confidently, but much of the extra is wrong.[fn:hypnosis-recall]
  Researchers who studied hypnotic past-life regression found that the "past lives" follow what the subjects were told to expect
  (Spanos and colleagues, 1991).[fn:spanos]
- Shared storytellers: the reports that get passed on are chosen and written up by people who already hold the framework.

Consistency tells you there is a common cause. It doesn't tell you which one.
To tell a real other world apart from a shared script, you would need consistency that the script cannot explain:
details the person could not have known, checked afterwards, or the same reports from people who never heard the script.
That would be hard-to-vary evidence. Consistency alone is not.

* The one row we could check

Most rows in the table can never be checked. Nobody can visit the spirit world, or the elf realm, and come back with a photo.
But there is one more row with the same kind of evidence where reality /did/ get a vote.

| Who                           | Experience                                | Therefore                                       |
|-------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------|
| a patient in therapy          | recovers memories of being raped as a child | my father raped me                            |

In the 1980s and 90s, "recovered memory" therapy, using techniques such as hypnosis, guided imagery and suggestive questioning,
led many people to vivid, detailed, emotionally overwhelming memories of childhood sexual abuse.[fn:recovered-memory]
Some of these could be checked, and turned out never to have happened.

- Beth Rutherford's church counselor taught her to go into self-hypnotic trance states. She came out of it "remembering" that her father, a minister, had raped her,
  and that she had gone through a coat-hanger abortion. A gynecological exam later showed she was still a virgin, and her father had had a vasectomy.[fn:rutherford]
  In 1996 the church and the counselor settled for $1 million.
- Paul Ingram, accused by his daughters, came to "remember" and confess the abuse after long, suggestive interrogations.
  The sociologist Richard Ofshe then made up a scene that nobody had ever alleged, told Ingram about it, and Ingram came back with a detailed written confession of it.[fn:ingram]
- In the lab, in a small study, Elizabeth Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell got about a quarter of their subjects to remember, at least partly,
  being lost in a shopping mall as a child. It never happened.[fn:loftus]

To be clear: sexual abuse is real and common, and most survivors have always remembered it.[fn:survivors]
Some do forget and remember later. It was the memories produced by suggestion in therapy that turned out to be unreliable.
The point is not about them. The point is about the evidence class.
These memories could feel just as real as the true ones: vivid, detailed, emotional, held with confidence. As consistent as any report in the table.[fn:felt-real]
They destroyed families.

So: if "I remembered it under hypnosis" were a source of knowledge, these memories would be knowledge too.
The one time this kind of evidence could be held against reality, reality said no.

* The worst case

Kant's maxim tests a rule by universalizing it: could I will that everybody acts on it?
For evidence I like to go one step further and look straight at the worst case:
what is the worst thing somebody could legitimately claim with this kind of evidence?

For /meaningful experience in an altered state/ the worst case is unbounded.
It licenses any cult leader, any god, any planet of origin, any contradiction.
An evidence class whose worst case is unbounded is no evidence class.

* Why I like this move

- You never have to tell anybody their experience was fake. It wasn't.
- You never have to argue about anybody's method. The false memories are a bonus row, not the foundation: the argument works without them.
- It works on any claim of this form, and you can make the list yourself in five minutes.
- It is symmetric. It applies to me.
  I have had experiences in meditation that /felt/ like insight into the nature of things.
  They are interesting. They tell me something about my brain.
  If I want to rescue anything spiritual from them, it has to be the kind of thing that survives criticism
  (see [[denote:20251026T183929][Recovering Some Spiritual Concepts]]).

The general rule:

#+begin_quote
Experiences of the special, meaningful, overwhelming kind can't be the basis of an ontology.
Before you trust a kind of evidence, look at everybody else it would make right.
#+end_quote
